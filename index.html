Pipelines and orchestration

Replacing a six-hour manual report with a scheduled pipeline

Marketplace analytics · 2026

The problem

The weekly OKR pack was assembled by hand every Monday. Someone exported tickets from Jira, pasted them into a workbook, reconciled the totals against the warehouse, and rebuilt the charts. It took most of a morning, it broke whenever a field was renamed upstream, and it only ran if one particular laptop happened to be switched on.

What I built

An ELT chain with no manual step in the middle. The Jira export lands in cloud storage, an external table exposes it to the warehouse without a load job, and a parameterized notebook handles the transformation and joins it to delivery data. The whole run is scheduled and alerts on failure. I made it idempotent, so re-running a bad week repairs the data instead of double-counting it.

What changed

Six hours down to nine minutes. The report has landed on time for eighteen consecutive weeks, including two when I was on leave.

Data quality and governance

Building a KPI reference that two teams could agree on

Marketplace analytics · 2026

The problem

The new-customer flag looked authoritative, but it applied a one-year look-back inside a two-year history window. A customer who last purchased fourteen months earlier was counted as new, and anyone dormant beyond the window was misclassified without anyone noticing. None of it was written down. Separately, the word "service" referred to both a category of work and an individual job, depending on who was using it.

What I built

A KPI reference and data dictionary covering every reported metric, each with its formula, source table, grain, owner, and reporting cadence. Terms carrying more than one meaning are flagged explicitly, and any metric affected by a window constraint carries a note explaining what it does and does not include. I added layer-level quality checks so gaps surface on a dashboard rather than in a meeting.

What changed

One definition per metric, caveats that travel with the number into executive reporting, and a standing quality view that catches a broken feed before a stakeholder does.

Analysis

Identifying retrieval, not listing quality, as the conversion constraint

Marketplace search · 2026

The problem

Conversion on a regional marketplace had been flat for two quarters. The prevailing theory was listing quality: weak titles, thin descriptions, missing photos. The proposed response was a seller education program that would have taken most of a quarter to run.

What I did

Before testing that theory, I checked whether listings were being shown at all. I ranked every active listing by views, split the catalog into deciles, and plotted the cumulative distribution. The top decile accounted for 29 percent of all views, while the bottom half accounted for 1.2 percent between them. At a Gini coefficient of 0.71, retrieval is the binding constraint rather than content. No amount of rewriting improves a listing the engine never surfaces.

What changed

The program was redirected from seller education to retrieval coverage, with a named owner for the listings that had never appeared in a result set.

Instrumentation design

Designing event tracking for an internal AI tool at launch

Energy utility · 2025

The problem

The tool shipped to roughly 4,000 staff with three features behind a single interface: chat, document analysis, and enterprise search. Errors surfaced as one generic message. Leadership could see usage rising and complaints rising, with no way to connect the two.

What I built

An event taxonomy with a consistent parameter set across every event, and one deliberate addition: failure events record which feature failed rather than a generic error flag. Events key to the corporate directory ID, so usage joins to department in SQL without collecting anything new about individual staff.

What changed

Failure rates became attributable by feature within two weeks. The largest single cause turned out to be a timeout on large document uploads, a fix that had gone unprioritized because nobody could size it.
